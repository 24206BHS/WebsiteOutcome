The birds of Canterbury

Pīwakawaka

The pīwakawaka (fantail) is one of New Zealand’s most widespread and adaptable native insectivores, maintaining a resilient population across the Canterbury region. Characterised by its distinctive fan-shaped tail and erratic, agile flight, this small passerine utilizes its extreme maneuverability to capture invertebrates mid-air. In Canterbury, the species thrives across a diverse range of habitats, spanning native beech forests in Arthur's Pass, coastal scrub on the Banks Peninsula, and urban green spaces across Christchurch.

In Māori tradition, the pīwakawaka holds deep cultural and spiritual significance. It is widely regarded as a messenger between the physical and spiritual realms. In traditional oral narratives, the bird's distinctive movements and interactions with humans carry specific omens or insights. It is famously featured in the mythological exploits of the demigod Māui, where its actions played a pivotal role in the origin of mortality for humankind. This rich cultural legacy, combined with the bird's highly visible and fearless nature, makes it a keystone species in regional environmental identity.

Kererū

The kererū (New Zealand wood pigeon) is New Zealand’s sole surviving native pigeon and plays a critical ecological role as the region's primary seed disperser. Characterised by its large size, iridescent green and copper plumage, and distinctive white breast, this heavy bird is easily identified by the loud, rushing sound of its wings in flight. Within Canterbury, populations can be observed in podocarp forest remnants, regenerating bush on Banks Peninsula, and urban sanctuaries like Riccarton Bush in Christchurch.

In Māori culture, the kererū is a highly treasured taonga (treasure). Historically, it was a vital seasonal food source and its feathers were highly prized for weaving prestigious kahu kererū (feather cloaks). In traditional narratives, the bird's beautiful iridescent colouring is attributed to the demigod Māui, who hid his mother's skirt to delay her, later transforming himself into a pigeon wearing the garment to follow her into the underworld. Because the kererū is the only remaining native bird with a gape large enough to swallow the large fruit of key native trees like karaka, tawa, and taraire, its presence is fundamental to the long-term survival of Canterbury's indigenous forests.

The korimako (New Zealand bellbird) is a widespread endemic honeyeater that plays a crucial ecological role as a key pollinator and seed disperser across Waitaha (Canterbury). Characterised by its olive-green plumage, curved bill, and striking wine-red eyes, this agile bird is easily recognized by its loud, whirring flight and melodious, bell-like song. In Canterbury, the species is highly adaptable and thrives across a range of habitats, from the dense beech forests of the foothills and regenerating bush on the Port Hills to urban sanctuaries and suburban gardens across Christchurch.

In Māori culture, the korimako is a deeply revered taonga (treasure), widely celebrated as a supreme symbol of oratorical and musical eloquence. The exceptional quality of its voice earned it a prominent place in traditional whakataukī (proverbs), where a flawless singer or powerful speaker is likened to a bellbird singing at dawn. Historically, the bird also held a sacred spiritual role in traditional baptismal rites, where its presence was used to symbolically impart eloquence to newborn infants before being released. This rich cultural legacy, combined with the bird’s unforgettable contribution to the regional dawn chorus, makes it a cornerstone of Canterbury’s environmental identity.
